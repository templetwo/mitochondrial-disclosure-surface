## Abstract

The outer mitochondrial membrane is traditionally viewed as a passive barrier or a binary pore during cell death. We propose a reframing in which the outer membrane is an active *disclosure surface* whose physical state vector continuously publishes mitochondrial condition to the cytosol. Central to this model is a sequential-sensitization spine in which VDAC1 oligomerization functions as a single upstream axis gating downstream cellular responses. As stress escalates, progressive VDAC1 oligomerization and N-terminal helix exposure move the membrane through distinct regimes: metabolic exchange, mitophagic containment via cardiolipin externalization, inflammatory alarm via sublethal mtDNA release, and terminal apoptosis. Building on recent structural evidence that the exposed VDAC1 N-terminus occupies the BH3-binding groove of Bcl-xL, we propose that sustained N-terminal exposure neutralizes Bcl-xL and derepresses BAX/BAK to form macropores — providing a unified mechanistic link between inflammatory and apoptotic disclosure. To distinguish this sequential model from independent parallel-attractor models, we outline a minimal multiplexed experiment tracking VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL co-occupancy alongside mtDNA and cytochrome *c* release, with explicit falsification criteria. This framework repositions VDAC1 as a context-dependent witness-node integrating local lipid, redox, and partner signals.

## Introduction

The textbook image of the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) is a semi-permeable wrapper that becomes a discrete binary gate during terminal events such as apoptosis: sealed in health, ruptured in death. This framing fails to capture the continuous, graded nature of mitochondrial signaling. The OMM has no default "sealed" state. In unstressed mitochondria it is constitutively permissive to ATP, ADP, NADH, and small metabolites conducted through the voltage-dependent anion channel VDAC1 in its high-conductance open state, and recent work shows that VDAC dimers additionally function as β-barrel phospholipid scramblases that mediate the bulk of lipid import into mitochondria (Jahn et al, 2023). The basal OMM is therefore not a closed wrapper but a low-bandwidth publication channel running continuously.

We propose that the OMM is more accurately modeled as an active **disclosure surface**: a physical layer whose continuously varying state vector — lipid packing, cardiolipin distribution, cholesterol content, partner occupancy, redox burden, mechanical tension, and contact-site geometry — dictates which class of mitochondrial cargo is published into the cytosol, and at what bandwidth. Stress does not open a membrane that was closed; it changes the class and bandwidth of what is already being published. In health, disclosure is low-bandwidth metabolic traffic. Under escalating stress, the interface shifts to disclose lipid asymmetry (externalized cardiolipin; Chu et al, 2013), then nucleic-acid fragments (mtDNA; West et al, 2015), and finally macromolecular pro-apoptotic cargo (cytochrome *c*).

At the center of this interface sits VDAC1, the most abundant OMM protein and the highest-bandwidth conduit between the intermembrane space and the cytosol. VDAC1 is a 19-stranded antiparallel β-barrel whose N-terminal α-helix folds inside the pore under resting conditions and translocates outward in response to voltage, ligand, lipid environment, and oligomeric state (Ujwal et al, 2008; Bayrhuber et al, 2008). Rather than a fixed-function channel or a single apoptotic pore, we reposition VDAC1 as a context-dependent **witness-node** whose function is set, at any moment, by its current partner network — hexokinase-II, tubulin, Bcl-xL, the IP3R–GRP75 calcium bridge, and the surrounding lipid domain (Rostovtseva et al, 2008; Szabadkai et al, 2006; Lafargue et al, 2025). On this view, the context-dependent pharmacology of VDAC1-targeting compounds is not a failure of selectivity but a prediction of partner-dependent function.

## Results and Discussion

### A sequential-sensitization spine, not parallel attractors

Current models often treat the stress regimes of the OMM — mitophagic containment, inflammatory mtDNA alarm, and apoptotic commitment — as parallel attractors with distinct pore architectures: VDAC1 oligomeric pores for alarm versus BAX/BAK macropores for commitment, addressed through mechanistically separate routes. We propose instead a unifying mechanism: a **sequential-sensitization spine** in which VDAC1 oligomerization is a single upstream axis whose amplitude and duration set the downstream consequence.

VDAC1 exists in a dynamic monomer–dimer equilibrium that shifts toward trimers, tetramers, and higher-order rings under apoptotic stimulation, in a manner that is concentration-, lipid-, and partner-dependent (Keinan et al, 2010; Lafargue et al, 2025). Driven by stress-induced lipid changes — cardiolipin externalization and oxidation (Kagan et al, 2005) and shifting partner occupancy — oligomerization exposes the VDAC1 N-terminal helix at the cytosolic face. The mechanistic keystone of this proposal is recent structural evidence that the exposed N-terminus (VDAC1-N) forms a complex with the BH3-binding groove of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-xL, acting as a sensitizer-type BH3 ligand that promotes BAK pore formation by neutralizing Bcl-xL (Daniilidis et al, 2025). By occupying that groove, VDAC1-N derepresses BAX/BAK and lowers the threshold for their oligomerization into mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) pores. On this reading, VDAC1 oligomerization is not a parallel pore competing with BAX/BAK; it is the upstream event that gates BAX/BAK activation. The four regimes collapse from parallel attractors into graded outcomes along one escalating disclosure program.

This sequence is consistent with the established downstream architecture of permeabilization. Following BAX/BAK-dependent MOMP, the outer-membrane pores widen until the inner membrane herniates into the cytosol and permeabilizes, releasing mtDNA that engages the cGAS–STING pathway (McArthur et al, 2018; Riley et al, 2018). The spine model places VDAC1 oligomerization upstream of this cascade and proposes that sublethal oligomerization can disclose short mtDNA fragments — driving type I interferon responses while the cell survives (West et al, 2015) — before the VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL crossover commits the cell to terminal MOMP.

### The five-state spectrum

The spine resolves into five states along a single axis of increasing stress amplitude (Table 1). The transition of consequence is the crossing from State 3 (partial, survivable inflammatory disclosure) to State 4 (the sensitization threshold at which VDAC1-N engages the Bcl-xL groove), which we model as a separatrix crossing in state space rather than a discrete decision. Containment is actively maintained upstream of this crossing: Parkin ubiquitinates VDAC1 at Lys53, restraining oligomerization and limiting mtDNA release, which positions mitophagy and apoptosis as antagonistic outcomes of the same node (Ham et al, 2020).

### A falsifiable minimal multiplexed experiment

The sequential and parallel-attractor models make divergent, testable predictions. We propose a stress titration in a cell system where OMM state is the load-bearing variable — for example, cancer cells with high VDAC1/HK-II dependence, with matched normal or VDAC1-edited controls — measuring the full state vector in parallel at single-cell or single-mitochondrion resolution across a dose–response and timecourse.

The spine predicts a specific temporal ordering (Figure 3): (1) VDAC1 oligomerization rises above baseline; (2) short-fragment mtDNA appears in the cytosol with concomitant cGAS–STING activation; (3) VDAC1 N-terminal exposure crosses a threshold; (4) Bcl-xL occupancy shifts from BH3-only proteins to VDAC1-N; (5) BAK/BAX conformational activation rises sharply; (6) cytochrome *c* and full-length mtDNA release. The alarm-to-commitment transition is marked by the VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL crossover. The parallel-attractor alternative predicts looser ordering, no required crossover, and reproducibly findable conditions producing robust mtDNA alarm without subsequent commitment.

**Three outcomes would falsify the spine:** (i) mtDNA alarm in conditions where VDAC1 N-terminal exposure is undetectable; (ii) VDAC1-N exposure without subsequent change in Bcl-xL/BAK occupancy; (iii) BAX/BAK activation reproducibly preceding any rise in VDAC1 oligomerization. **Three would support it:** (i) VDAC1 oligomerization rising before mtDNA alarm in every titrated condition; (ii) VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL co-occupancy rising sharply at the alarm-to-commitment transition; (iii) genetic or pharmacological prevention of VDAC1-N exposure abrogating BAX/BAK activation without abolishing mtDNA alarm.

### Pharmacological corollaries and the re-audit imperative

The partner-dependent, single-axis model carries direct pharmacological consequences. First, it predicts two-register drug behavior: a VDAC1-targeting compound produces VDAC1-dependent effects where OMM disclosure is load-bearing, but acts through other mechanisms where the load-bearing variable lies elsewhere — exactly the pattern seen for cannabidiol, which kills VDAC1-high cancer cells through a membrane-level mechanism yet rescues Leigh-syndrome models through PPARγ activation independent of VDAC1 (Puighermanal et al, 2024). Second, the model identifies VDAC1 oligomerization as a therapeutic axis in its own right: cyathin-R drives VDAC1 oligomerization sufficient to release cytochrome *c* and induce apoptosis even in BAX/BAK-deficient cells (Huang et al, 2015), a useful boundary case showing that under strong pharmacological pressure oligomerization can drive terminal release without BAX/BAK. Third, the model demands caution about tool-compound specificity: a recent biophysical audit reports that VBIT-4, used for years as a selective VDAC1 oligomerization inhibitor, partitions into and destabilizes lipid bilayers independently of VDAC1 (Ravishankar et al, 2025, *preprint*). If this finding holds under peer review and replication, causal claims attributing therapeutic effects specifically to VDAC1 oligomerization inhibition on the basis of VBIT-4 alone require re-evaluation. We flag this explicitly as a claim still awaiting peer-reviewed confirmation.

### Limitations

This is a synthesis-and-hypothesis framework, not a primary-data study. The keystone VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL interaction (Daniilidis et al, 2025) requires attention to cell system and concentration regime and independent replication before being generalized as the unifying mechanism; if it proves context-limited, the framework reverts to the parallel-attractor model. Lateral lipid organization of the native OMM remains technically difficult to measure in living cells, and several state-vector variables are presently inferential. The proposed experiment is the intended test of whether the framework is load-bearing.

## Methods

The following describes how each variable of the proposed multiplexed experiment would be measured. No primary data are reported here; this section specifies the protocol blueprint that operationalizes the framework's predictions.

**VDAC1 oligomeric state.** Oligomer distribution (monomer through higher-order rings) is resolved by chemical crosslinking of isolated OMM or intact cells with a membrane-permeant crosslinker (e.g., EGS or DSS) followed by native or SDS-PAGE and anti-VDAC1 immunoblot, with crosslinker-free and monomer-only controls. Single-particle distributions are confirmed in reconstituted OMM by high-speed atomic force microscopy and, where feasible, cryo-electron tomography of isolated organelles, against lipid-matched bilayers to control for lipid-driven assembly artifacts (Lafargue et al, 2025).

**VDAC1 N-terminal helix exposure.** Cytosolic-face exposure of the N-terminal helix is detected with conformation-specific antibodies and, in parallel, an intramolecular FRET pair reporting helix displacement, validated against oligomerization-deficient and constitutively exposed mutants.

**Bcl-xL occupancy.** The shift of Bcl-xL binding from BH3-only proteins to VDAC1-N is quantified by co-immunoprecipitation under digitonin extraction and by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) or targeted mass spectrometry, with competition controls using BH3-mimetics to confirm groove-specific binding.

**BAK/BAX activation.** Conformational activation is detected with epitope-specific antibodies (e.g., recognizing the activated BAK/BAX N-terminus) by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence, with BAX/BAK-double-knockout cells as negative controls.

**Cytosolic mtDNA.** Digitonin fractionation separates cytosolic from organellar compartments; size-resolved droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) with fragment-spanning and short-amplicon probes distinguishes short fragments (sublethal disclosure) from full-length circles (post-commitment release), normalized to nuclear-DNA and whole-cell mtDNA controls.

**cGAS–STING activation.** Pathway engagement is read out as 2′3′-cGAMP levels (LC-MS or ELISA), IRF3 phosphorylation (phospho-flow or immunoblot), and an interferon-stimulated-gene (ISG) signature by qPCR or RNA-seq.

**Cytochrome *c* release.** Release fraction is measured by immunoblot of subcellular fractions and confirmed in live cells with a fluorescent cytochrome-*c* reporter.

**Cardiolipin externalization and oxidation.** Outer-leaflet cardiolipin is tracked with cardiolipin-binding probes (NAO or annexin-style probes) by flow cytometry; oxidation state, mechanistically distinct from externalization, is quantified by oxidative lipidomics (LC-MS) detecting cardiolipin hydroperoxide species (Kagan et al, 2005).

**Membrane tension and ER–OMM distance.** Where instrumentation permits, mitochondrial tension is estimated with force-sensing biosensors and ER–OMM contact-site distance by FIB-SEM or live-cell super-resolution microscopy.

**Network permeabilization fraction.** The proportion of the mitochondrial network undergoing permeabilization is quantified by live-cell imaging to capture minority-MOMP and survivable-threshold behavior.

Variables are acquired across a graded stress dose–response and timecourse; temporal ordering and the predicted VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL crossover are tested against the falsification criteria above.

| State | VDAC1 oligomeric state | Membrane condition | Cellular consequence |
|-------|------------------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| **1. Ordinary exchange** | Monomer–dimer equilibrium; HK-II / Bcl-xL stabilized | Open conducting; ordinary lipid asymmetry | Low-bandwidth metabolic publication ignored by signaling |

## Figure Legends

**Figure 1. From binary gate to disclosure surface.** Schematic contrasting the textbook model, in which the OMM transitions discretely from a sealed wrapper (health) to a ruptured terminal state (apoptosis), with the proposed disclosure-surface model, in which a single escalating program moves the membrane through graded regimes — metabolic exchange, lipid-asymmetry strain, mtDNA alarm, and BAX/BAK-dependent commitment — as a function of stress amplitude.

**Figure 2. The sequential-sensitization spine.** Mechanistic schematic of the proposed single-axis model. Increasing stress and lipid changes drive VDAC1 oligomerization (trimers/tetramers), which produces sublethal mtDNA leak and cGAS–STING activation; sustained stress crosses a sensitization threshold at which the exposed VDAC1 N-terminal helix occupies the Bcl-xL BH3 groove, neutralizing Bcl-xL, derepressing BAX/BAK, and enabling macropore formation and cytochrome *c* release. Arrows denote proposed causal sequence, not parallel branches.

**Figure 3. Predicted temporal ordering across a stress titration.** Timecourse prediction distinguishing the sequential model from parallel-attractor alternatives. Upstream sensitization (VDAC1 oligomerization, then N-terminal exposure) precedes alarm cargo (cytosolic mtDNA, cGAS–STING), which precedes the critical VDAC1-N/Bcl-xL binding event, which in turn precedes terminal commitment (BAX/BAK activation, cytochrome *c*/MOMP). The relative onset and overlap of these tracks constitute the experiment's discriminating readout.
